# Unit 1.1: Acoustical Awareness & The Anatomy of Sound

**Week 1 · Introductory high-school music**  
**Teacher:** Goblin Bard, he/him  
**Format:** direct instruction, listening demonstrations, observation, discussion and written or spoken explanation.

## Essential question

How can we describe what we hear, and how can a musician use those qualities intentionally?

## Learning objectives

By the end of the introductory lesson, students should be able to:

1. Describe a sound using pitch, loudness, duration and timbre.
2. Explain why noise is a kind of sound, and why a sound considered noise in one context can become musical material in another.
3. Compare instruments playing the same note and identify timbre as a major source of their different sound qualities.
4. Support a listening observation with a specific audible detail.

## Key concepts to teach

### Sound, noise and musical intention

Sound travels through a medium such as air as vibrations. The term **noise** has several meanings: unwanted sound in everyday use, and particular kinds of irregular or random signals in acoustics. It is not a separate category outside sound. Whether a sound is welcome or unwanted depends partly on its context. See [Simon Fraser University's acoustic terminology handbook: Noise](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/handbook/Noise.html).

Musicians can select and organize sounds through timing, repetition, contrast and layering. A closing door, traffic or rustling paper can become material for a composition. Avoid teaching a fixed division in which musical sounds are always orderly and noises are always random: pitched music may be unwanted, while percussion and deliberately noisy textures may be part of music.

**Teacher explanation:**

> Sound is our material. A sound might interrupt you in one setting and be exactly what a composer wants in another. Today we will listen for its properties before deciding what we might do with it.

### Four properties of sound

| Property | Plain-language explanation | Demonstration and question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pitch — related to frequency** | How high or low a sound seems. For a steady musical tone, its fundamental frequency helps determine its pitch; frequency is measured in hertz, or cycles per second. Some sounds have no clear single pitch. | Play a low and a high note at a similar listening level. “Which changed: high/low or loud/soft?” |
| **Loudness / dynamics — related to amplitude** | How loud or soft a sound seems. Amplitude describes the strength of a vibration; loudness is a perception, influenced by more than amplitude alone. A playback volume control changes the listening level. | Play the same recorded tone at two comfortable levels. “Did it become a higher note, or a louder version of the same note?” |
| **Duration — time** | How long a sound lasts. It can be estimated in seconds or, later, described with musical note values. Duration is different from tempo, the rate of the beat. | Compare a short and a sustained version of one note. “What stayed the same?” |
| **Timbre — tone color** | The quality that helps us distinguish sounds even when their pitch and loudness are similar. It depends on frequency content and how the sound begins, develops and fades. | Compare a flute and violin on the same note. “How would you describe the difference without just naming the instrument?” |

The distinction between physical properties and perception is supported by the [NIH curriculum's explanation of hearing](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK20366/). Timbre includes both spectral and time-varying characteristics; see [SFU's Timbre entry](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/handbook/Timbre.html).

## First-day lesson: approximately 30 minutes

### 1. Welcome and listening question — 2 minutes

Introduce the essential question and explain what students will learn. Ask students to name a sound they noticed on the way to class. Use one answer to introduce the idea that everyday listening is a starting point for studying music.

### 2. Explain and demonstrate the four properties — 6 minutes

Use the demonstrations in the table. Change one property at a time where practical. Model a complete observation: **“The note became louder, but I did not hear its pitch change.”** Keep definitions visible during the later listening work.

### 3. Soundscape Mapping — 3 minutes listening, 4 minutes discussion

Students sit quietly and listen to the room for three minutes, cataloging ambient sounds. They may jot brief notes or use a prepared observation sheet. Silence from the class creates space to hear the environment; it does not require a silent room.

Suggested sheet:

| Sound / likely source | Pitch | Duration / recurrence | Loudness | Timbre description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ventilation | Low or no clear single pitch | Continuous | Soft | Airy, diffuse |
| Footsteps outside | No clear single pitch | Short, repeated | Moderate | Dull, hollow |
| Bird outside | Relatively high | Short, intermittent | Soft | Bright, whistling |

These rows are illustrative; students record what they actually hear. Relative descriptions are acceptable. Do not require an exact frequency or force every sound into a high/low category when it lacks a clear pitch.

Discuss two or three observations. Ask: **“Which sound did you hear continuously? Which had a clear pitch? What detail supports your description?”**

Then ask students to describe how a composer could arrange two of these sounds using repetition, contrast, order or layering. A short written proposal is enough; producing a composition is not required for this lesson.

### 4. The Timbre Experiment — 10 minutes

**Prepare:** four short recordings of the same sounding pitch in the same octave, played on violin, synthesizer, flute and distorted guitar. For example, use a sustained A4 on all four. Match duration and perceived playback loudness as closely as practical, and use the same listening setup. Exact matching is an instructional aim; acknowledge obvious differences rather than attributing every difference to instrument identity.

1. Explain what will stay approximately constant: pitch, duration and listening loudness. Identify timbre as the main quality being compared.
2. Play each example with the instrument identified. This is a comparison, not an instrument-guessing task.
3. Model a description using audible features: **“This example starts sharply and fades quickly; this one has a breathy beginning and a more sustained tone.”** Use descriptions that match the actual selected clips.
4. Students make a visual tone-color map using words, lines, shapes or colors. Offer descriptive axes such as **smooth–rough** and **soft-edged–sharp-edged**. Each placement needs a written or spoken reason.
5. Replay the examples and discuss disagreements. Color choices are personal associations, not a universal code or an objective acoustic measurement.
6. Connect the observation to music: **“Why might a composer choose this sound for a particular passage?”** Require a sound-based explanation rather than a fixed claim that an instrument always creates one emotion.

Explain that a note can have the same fundamental pitch while its overtones and attack/decay differ. Distortion changes the guitar's frequency content, making it a useful contrast. A synthesizer can produce many timbres; the selected patch is one example, not the synthesizer's only sound.

### 5. Check understanding — 5 minutes

Ask students to answer in writing or speech:

1. What is the difference between a higher note and a louder note?
2. Two instruments play the same note at a similar loudness. What property helps explain why they sound different? Give one detail you heard.
3. Describe one ambient sound by pitch and duration, including “no clear pitch” if appropriate.
4. Could a sound considered noise become part of music? Explain a possible use.

**Look for:** high/low distinguished from loud/soft; timbre named with listening evidence; a credible observation of duration; and a context-based explanation of sound/noise. Correct misunderstandings with another demonstration. Do not assess speed, instrument identification or artistic drawing skill.

## Continue through Week 1

Adjust the number of meetings to the school timetable. Subsequent lessons can deepen:

- **Frequency and amplitude:** compare controlled tones and read simple waveforms with labeled axes. Relate each change to what students hear.
- **Timbre and sound development:** compare attacks, sustained portions, decays and simple spectra of the selected recordings.
- **Musical organization:** describe or annotate a short arrangement of environmental sounds, explaining choices with the unit vocabulary.

The first lesson introduces the vocabulary and listening habits; later lessons develop their scientific and musical detail.
